def question1(repetitions,n,p):
    """
    nsim : number of simulation
    n: number of sample
    p : number pf covariate
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(43202)

    #generate X_all data drawn from N(0,1) and intercept
    X = rng.normal(0, 1, size=(n, p))
    X_all = np.column_stack((np.ones(n), X))
    
    sigma = 1.0
    covariate_count = np.arange(p + 1)

    #let β_j=0.4j
    beta = np.zeros(p + 1)
    beta[1:] = 0.4 ** np.sqrt(np.arange(1, p + 1))

    mu = X_all @ beta
    
    train_mse_decay = np.empty((repetitions, p+1))
    test_mse_decay = np.empty((repetitions, p+1))
    for re in range(repetitions):
        ## genenrate training and test reponse 
        y_train = mu + rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=sigma, size=n)
        y_test = mu + rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=sigma, size=n)
        ## fit for the intercep model
        y_hat = np.repeat(y_train.mean(), n)
        train_mse_decay[re, 0] = np.mean((y_train - y_hat) ** 2)
        test_mse_decay[re, 0] = np.mean((y_test - y_hat) ** 2)
        for m in range(1, p+1):
            X_m = X_all[:, :m+1]
            beta_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(X_m, y_train, rcond=None)[0]
            y_hat = X_m @ beta_hat
            train_mse_decay[re, m] = np.mean((y_train - y_hat) ** 2)
            test_mse_decay[re, m] = np.mean((y_test - y_hat) ** 2)
    


    bias_mse_decay = np.empty(p + 1)
    bias_mse_decay[0] = np.mean((mu - (mu).mean()) ** 2)

    for m in range(1, p + 1):
        X_m = X_all[:, :m + 1]
        beta_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(X_m, mu, rcond=None)[0]
        y_hat = X_m @ beta_hat
        bias_mse_decay[m] = np.mean((mu - y_hat) ** 2)
    
    theory_train_mse_decay = bias_mse_decay + (1 - (covariate_count + 1) / n) * sigma**2
    theory_test_mse_decay = bias_mse_decay + (1 + (covariate_count + 1) / n) * sigma**2
    return train_mse_decay,test_mse_decay,theory_train_mse_decay,theory_test_mse_decay

c.2 $$ E(\text{MSE}_{\text{train}} \mid X) = \frac{B^2}{n} + \left(1 - \frac{p}{n}\right)\sigma^2 $$ where:

  • $B^2$ is the sum of squared model biases (i.e., the sum of squared differences between the fitted function and the true function; if the model is correctly specified, then $B^2 = 0$);
  • $p$ the total number of model parameters;
  • $n$ is the sample size;
  • $\sigma^2$ is the variance of the error term. As the number of predictors $p$ increases:

(1) The variance term $\left(1 - \frac{p}{n}\right)\sigma^2$ monotonically decreases (because $1 - \frac{p}{n}$ becomes smaller).

(2) The bias term $\frac{B^2}{n}$ does not increase, since adding more variables may capture additional true signal, and even if they are not, the bias at least remains unchanged.

Question 3: The optimism correction¶

1. Derive Expected Training MSE, Expected Test MSE, and the Expected Optimism¶

(1) Expected Training MSE¶

We have

$$ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{y} &= \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}) \\ &= (\boldsymbol{I}_n - \boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{\mu} + (\boldsymbol{I}_n - \boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}. \end{aligned} $$

Taking the squared norm and expectation, the cross term

$$ 2(\boldsymbol{I}_n-\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{\mu} \cdot (\boldsymbol{I}_n-\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{\epsilon} $$

has zero expectation because

$$ E(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\mid\boldsymbol{X})=0. $$

Thus,

$$ \begin{aligned} E(\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{train}}\mid\boldsymbol{X}) &= \frac{1}{n} \left[ \left\| (\boldsymbol{I}_n-\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{\mu} \right\|^2 + E\left( \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\top} (\boldsymbol{I}_n-\boldsymbol{H}) \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \right) \right] \\ &= \frac{B^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{I}_n-\boldsymbol{H}) \\ &= \frac{B^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \left(n-(p+1)\right) \\ &= \boxed{ \frac{B^2}{n} + \sigma^2 \left( 1-\frac{p+1}{n} \right) }. \end{aligned} $$

(2) Expected Test MSE¶

We have

$$ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{y}^*-\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{y} &= \boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^* - \boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}) \\ &= (\boldsymbol{I}_n-\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^* - \boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}. \end{aligned} $$

All cross terms have zero expectation because $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*$ are independent and have mean zero.

Thus,

$$ \begin{aligned} E(\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{test}}\mid\boldsymbol{X}) &= \frac{1}{n} \left[ B^2 + E\left(\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*\|^2\right) + E\left( \|\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^2 \right) \right] \\ &= \frac{B^2}{n} + \frac{n\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{H})}{n} \\ &= \frac{B^2}{n} + \sigma^2 + \frac{(p+1)\sigma^2}{n} \\ &= \boxed{ \frac{B^2}{n} + \sigma^2 \left( 1+\frac{p+1}{n} \right) }. \end{aligned} $$

(3) Expected Optimism¶

The expected optimism is

$$ \begin{aligned} E( \mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{test}} - \mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{train}} \mid\boldsymbol{X} ) &= \left( 1+\frac{p+1}{n} \right)\sigma^2 - \left( 1-\frac{p+1}{n} \right)\sigma^2 \\ &= \boxed{ \frac{2(p+1)\sigma^2}{n} }. \end{aligned} $$

2. Numerical Computation for $n=120$, $p=4$, $\frac{B^2}{n}=0.04$, and $\sigma^2=1$¶

Here,

$$ p+1=5. $$

Expected Training MSE¶

$$ \begin{aligned} E(\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{train}}) &= 0.04+ \left( 1-\frac{5}{120} \right)\times 1 \\ &= 0.04+\frac{115}{120} \\ &= 0.04+0.9583 \\ &= \boxed{0.9983}. \end{aligned} $$

Expected Test MSE¶

$$ \begin{aligned} E(\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{test}}) &= 0.04+ \left( 1+\frac{5}{120} \right)\times 1 \\ &= 0.04+\frac{125}{120} \\ &= 0.04+1.0417 \\ &= \boxed{1.0817}. \end{aligned} $$

Expected Difference (Optimism)¶

$$ \begin{aligned} E( \mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{test}} - \mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{train}} ) &= \frac{2\times5\times1}{120} \\ &= \frac{10}{120} \\ &= \boxed{0.0833}. \end{aligned} $$

Why the Ordering Need Not Hold for Every Realized Pair¶

The above values are expectations, meaning they are averages over many repeated samples.

For a single realized pair of training and test responses:

  • The training noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ might be unusually small, making the realized training MSE much lower than its expectation.
  • The test noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*$ might be unusually large, making the realized test MSE much higher than its expectation.
  • It is therefore possible that, for one particular realization,

$$ \mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{train}} > \mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{test}}. $$

Only after averaging over many realizations does the positive expected optimism

$$ \frac{2(p+1)\sigma^2}{n} $$

become apparent.

3. Given $n=120$, $p=4$, $\mathrm{RSS}=116.4$, and $\hat{\sigma}^2=0.96$¶

We have

$$ p+1=5. $$

(1) Corrected Test MSE¶

The corrected estimate of the test MSE is

$$ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}} &= \frac{ \mathrm{RSS} + 2(p+1)\hat{\sigma}^2 }{n} \\ &= \frac{ 116.4 + 2\times5\times0.96 }{120} \\ &= \frac{116.4+9.6}{120} \\ &= \frac{126.0}{120} \\ &= \boxed{1.05}. \end{aligned} $$

(2) Mallows' $C_p$¶

Mallows' $C_p$ is

$$ C_p = \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\hat{\sigma}^2} - n + 2(p+1). $$

Substituting the given values,

$$ \begin{aligned} C_p &= \frac{116.4}{0.96} - 120 + 10 \\ &= 121.25-120+10 \\ &= \boxed{11.25}. \end{aligned} $$

(3) Relationship Between $\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}}$ and $C_p$¶

Starting from the corrected test MSE,

$$ \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}} = \frac{ \mathrm{RSS} + 2(p+1)\hat{\sigma}^2 }{n}. $$

Multiply both sides by $\frac{n}{\hat{\sigma}^2}$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{n}{\hat{\sigma}^2} \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}} &= \frac{ \mathrm{RSS} + 2(p+1)\hat{\sigma}^2 }{\hat{\sigma}^2} \\ &= \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\hat{\sigma}^2} + 2(p+1). \end{aligned} $$

Now recall that

$$ C_p = \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\hat{\sigma}^2} - n + 2(p+1). $$

Therefore,

$$ \begin{aligned} C_p &= \left( \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\hat{\sigma}^2} + 2(p+1) \right) - n \\ &= \frac{n}{\hat{\sigma}^2} \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}} - n. \end{aligned} $$

Hence,

$$ \boxed{ C_p = \frac{n}{\hat{\sigma}^2} \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}} - n }. $$

Equivalently,

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}} = \frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{n} (C_p+n) }. $$

(4) Why Minimizing $C_p$ Is Equivalent to Minimizing the Corrected Test MSE¶

When comparing a set of candidate models:

  • $n$ is the same for all candidate models.
  • $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is the same common estimate of the error variance for all candidate models.

Therefore,

$$ \frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{n} $$

is a positive constant across all candidate models.

From

$$ \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}} = \frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{n} (C_p+n), $$

we can see that $\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}}$ is a positive linear transformation of $C_p$.

Adding the constant $n$ and multiplying by the positive constant $\frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{n}$ do not change the ordering of the candidate models.

Therefore,

$$ \boxed{ \arg\min C_p = \arg\min \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}_{\mathrm{test}} }. $$

In other words:

The model that minimizes Mallows' $C_p$ also minimizes the corrected estimate of the test MSE.

This is the mathematical justification for using Mallows' $C_p$ as a model selection criterion.

c.3 Selecting a model only ranks a small set of candidate models under a particular criterion. It chooses the best model among those candidates, not the model that actually generated the data. The true data-generating model may not be in the candidate set at all, or it may involve omitted variables, nonlinearities, or interactions that none of the fitted candidates contain. Model selection is therefore a relative comparison, not a proof of the truth.

Question 5: Validation and final test data¶

a. Step 2 is the first misuse. Although the coefficients are fitted using only the training data, Steps 2-3 use the final test responses to compare and select among the candidate models. The test set therefore participates in model selection, so it is no longer an untouched final evaluation.

b. Each of the eleven test MSEs contains random variation. Taking the minimum tends to pick the model whose test MSE happened to be unusually small by chance. Reporting that same minimum as the final estimate is then overly optimistic: the number has already been "cherry-picked" from the eleven values, so it underestimates the true prediction error of the selected procedure.

c. A valid procedure is:

  1. Reserve rows 371-442 as the final test set and do not inspect their responses.
  2. Divide rows 1-370 into five folds.
  3. For each candidate predictor count m, train on four folds and compute the MSE on the remaining fold; repeat until every fold has served as the validation fold.
  4. Average the five validation MSEs for each m and select the m with the smallest average.
  5. Refit the selected model using all 370 training observations.
  6. Evaluate this fitted model once, only at the end, on rows 371-442.

def question5():
    data = pd.read_csv("../data/diabetes.csv")
    
    train = data.iloc[:370]
    n_train = train.shape[0]
    test = data.iloc[370:]
    variable_full = ['age', 'sex', 'bmi', 'bp', 's1', 's2', 's3', 's4', 's5', 's6']

        cv_mse[m] = np.mean(fold_mse)
    m_best = int(np.argmin(cv_mse))
